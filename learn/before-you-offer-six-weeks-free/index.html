Before you offer six weeks free, read this.

A concession can be the right response. First establish what is slowing leasing, who receives the offer, and what the improvement is worth after its full cost.

By Chris Miller, Founder and CEO, LeaserAbout 5 min read

In a minute.

  • Diagnose the barrier before changing the price.
  • Count the incentive cost for every lease that includes the offer.
  • Compare vacancy, concessions, and other costs over the same period.
In this article
  1. Find out what renters are actually declining.
  2. Specify the homes, terms, and approval.
  3. Count everyone who receives the incentive.
  4. Compare a small set of workable responses.
  5. Your practical tool

A nearby property advertises six weeks free. Your team has signed fewer leases than its target calls for. Matching the offer feels like a fast way to remove the objection.

Sometimes a stronger incentive is justified. But the offer needs to solve the actual problem. It cannot solve a shortage of tour appointments or make an apartment ready sooner.

Before approving a concession, ask which homes need it, what behavior it is expected to change, and how much rental income the property will retain under each alternative.

Find out what renters are actually declining.

Read recent lost-opportunity notes and confirm the details with the leasing team. Distinguish rent level, upfront cash requirements, unit fit, move-in timing, service concerns, and difficulty completing the process. Avoid treating every unanswered conversation as a pricing rejection.

Compare like-for-like homes and complete offer terms. A competitor’s headline may apply to a limited unit type, a different lease length, or a narrow move-in window. Mandatory fees and the timing of a credit also affect the renter’s actual choice.

If the evidence points to an operational problem, price may be an expensive substitute for fixing it. If comparable homes are materially more attractive after incentives and your pipeline is weakening, an offer change deserves a serious evaluation.

Specify the homes, terms, and approval.

Define the eligible inventory, lease terms, application or move-in deadlines, dollar value, and the person authorized to approve the offer. Apply published terms consistently and have the relevant team check legal, platform, and local requirements before launch.

State the actual credit. “Six weeks” can be implemented differently from “one and a half months” depending on the agreement and billing method. Use the approved dollar amount in the analysis instead of assuming the headline tells you the exact cost.

Show the total rental income over the lease and when payments are due. An upfront credit can affect near-term collections differently from a monthly reduction, even if the nominal total is similar.

Count everyone who receives the incentive.

If ten leases include the offer, the cost belongs to all ten. It is not enough to apply the concession only to the additional leases you hope to gain. Some of the ten may have signed under the previous terms.

Fannie Mae’s underwriting framework treats concessions as rental income forgone when evaluating property income. [1] The operating decision should likewise keep the entire incentive cost visible.

Estimate the additional move-ins or acceleration required to justify that cost. Separate the value of avoiding vacancy from other benefits, and do not count the same rent twice. Track whether the offer also displaces demand from another available home in the portfolio.

Illustrative example · assumptions, not customer results

A smaller offer can win—or lose—on timing.

Illustrative comparison: rent is $2,100 per month, represented as $70 per day using a 30-day month. Reducing an offer from 42 free-rent days to 28 saves $980 per lease. Assume all those credits are realized within the same comparison period and other terms remain unchanged.

If the smaller offer causes ten additional vacant days before the renter moves in, the extra vacancy represents $700 in rental income forgone. The modeled advantage is $280 before any difference in operating, marketing, or collection costs. If the delay is 18 days instead, the extra vacancy is $1,260 and the smaller offer is $280 worse.

Neither timing assumption is a forecast. The example identifies the question that decides the result: how much does the incentive actually change the move-in date? Use contract terms and real billing conventions for the property’s decision.

Compare a small set of workable responses.

Evaluate the current plan, a changed concession, and a credible operational or demand response. That alternative might include correcting price presentation, improving tour access, changing the available-home message, or redirecting approved media.

Use the same comparison period and include the full incremental cost of each response. Allow for different expected move-in dates, the chance of cancellation, unit readiness, and lease lengths. Record what changes at the end of the comparison period so a later start or shorter lease does not gain an artificial advantage.

Show what happens if fewer renters sign or move-ins take longer than expected. If the recommended option wins only when every assumption breaks in its favor, limit the initial commitment or gather better evidence before applying it widely.

Put it to work

The concession decision sheet.

Complete this before adding, extending, or removing an offer.

QuestionEvidence or decision to record
What is blocking leasing?Verified objections and stage-level conversion for the affected homes.
Which offer is comparable?Unit type, rent, fees, lease length, credit value, and timing.
Who receives it?All eligible leases, including those likely to sign without the change.
What changes?Additional move-ins or earlier rent payments, including a less favorable scenario.
What does it cost?Full incentives, extra vacancy, execution costs, and operational differences.
What is authorized?Offer scope, approver, owner, review date, and expiration or stop condition.

Keep the chosen terms in the same place the team checks current availability. An expired or inconsistent offer can create new friction after the analysis is complete.

The best offer is the one that improves the property’s economics under credible assumptions.

What a better result looks like.

Review achieved rent after concessions, move-ins and rent start dates, vacant days, and total incremental costs. Follow the leases far enough to see cancellations and actual credits. A rise in signed leases can still leave the property with less income if too many leases receive an unnecessary incentive or the offer costs too much.

Use realized results to revise the next offer. If recurring operating income improves, that may support asset value, but a short-term concession comparison is not by itself a stabilized NOI or valuation model. Keep that distinction clear in ownership reporting.
