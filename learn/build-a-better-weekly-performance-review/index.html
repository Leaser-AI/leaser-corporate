Build a better weekly performance review.

Turn the weekly meeting into a place where the team resolves the next operating decision—and gives ownership a clear account of the result.

By Chris Miller, Founder and CEO, LeaserAbout 6 min read

In a minute.

  • Use one reporting end date and a small set of defined measures.
  • Spend meeting time on the next decision and the evidence behind it.
  • Leave with an owner, a deadline, and a way to judge every action.
In this article
  1. Prepare one shared view.
  2. Open with the gap ahead.
  3. Bring a decision, with alternatives.

Marketing reports stronger engagement. Leasing reports more tours. The revenue team reports another change to the concession offer. Everyone has an update, but nobody can say whether the property is more likely to meet its next move-in target.

A useful weekly review connects these views before the meeting starts. Its job is to identify where rental income or occupancy could fall short of the target, choose a response, and make the next action clear.

The structure below is a proposed 30-minute working session. Adapt the timing to your portfolio, but preserve the discipline: prepare the facts beforehand and use the meeting to decide.

Prepare one shared view.

Use the same reporting end date and identify the source of each measure. Define a lead, a booked tour, a completed tour, a signed lease, and a move-in. State whether conversion reports follow the same group of prospects from inquiry to lease or simply count events occurring during the week. These are different views and should not be mixed.

Keep the preparation short enough to use. Include current occupied units, dated move-outs, scheduled move-ins, homes without signed leases, active prospect stages, acquisition spending, and concessions. Show collections or delinquency separately when relevant; a physically occupied home does not by itself describe the income collected.

Flag missing data, unusual adjustments, and dates that have not been updated. The team should know when it is deciding with provisional information rather than discovering the discrepancy after the budget changes.

Open with the gap ahead.

Begin with the move-ins needed over the next 30 to 60 days and the homes that create that need. Check the available-home list against property records before debating advertising results. Separate homes ready for move-in from homes that may take longer than expected to prepare or become available.

Then ask how much of the move-in target is covered by signed leases and how much relies on unsigned prospects. Show what happens if some signed leases are canceled or move-ins are delayed. A single confident percentage can conceal very different levels of certainty.

Do not let portfolio averages erase a property-specific problem. Strong performance elsewhere may create useful options, but it does not lease the homes that still need renters.

Bring a decision, with alternatives.

For each material issue, put one decision on the table. For example: should the team redirect an approved portion of spend, expand available tour times, revise the listing experience, or change an offer?

Describe the evidence, the proposed action, a realistic alternative, and what could happen if the team waits. State what needs approval and what the operating team can execute within existing authority. This gives ownership a decision they can assess rather than a list of activities they must interpret.

Keep uncertainty visible. If there are too few completed leases or the results are still arriving, reduce the initial commitment, allow more time, or run a test with a defined budget. Explain that uncertainty in the recommendation.

Illustrative example · assumptions, not customer results

The meeting changes when the question changes.

Illustrative operating scenario: a property has ten near-term two-bedroom openings. Four have signed leases; six still need renters. The team also has six active prospects seeking matching dates, but only two have confirmed tours. A request to increase media arrives alongside those facts.

The first decision is to verify the unbooked prospects’ needs and resolve any problems booking a suitable tour time. The leasing lead is responsible for follow-up and scheduling; marketing checks that the listing and landing page show those homes accurately. A spending increase stays available if the remaining pipeline cannot cover the gap.

At the next review, the team checks completed tours, applications, signed leases, and homes still without signed leases. It does not claim the problem is solved merely because follow-up messages were sent.

Put it to work

A 30-minute review that ends with a plan.

Use the agenda as a working template, not a mandatory meeting length.

TimeDecision output
0–5 minutesConfirm the reporting end date, changes in availability, and last week’s commitments.
5–13 minutesIdentify how many more move-ins are needed and which risks could change that number.
13–23 minutesChoose responses. Compare alternatives, expected costs, and uncertainty.
23–28 minutesAssign one accountable owner, an execution deadline, and required approvals.
28–30 minutesConfirm the result to review next week and the conditions for changing course.

Action record: Issue / evidence / chosen move / expected effect / approved cost / owner / due date / review measure / stop or escalation condition.

Give ownership the measures that answer its questions.

Ownership needs a clear view of what changed in the asset, why it changed, and what happens next. Lead with occupied homes and homes that are vacant or expected to become vacant, achieved rents and concessions, acquisition costs, and the effect on operating income when it can be measured. Keep estimated financial effects distinct from booked results.

Pair each problem with a proposed response. “Six two-bedrooms need renters within the next month; interested prospects cannot find suitable tour times; the leasing lead is adding appointments this week” is more actionable than a page of channel percentages.

Use operational leading indicators during the week, then check them against actual move-ins and the property’s financial results for the same period. Do not add overlapping benefits—for example, counting the same earlier move-in as both a full new lease and a separate vacancy saving.

A useful review ends with a decision someone can execute and a result someone will check.

Close the loop before opening another task.

At the following meeting, revisit the decision record. Was the action completed? Did the expected operating result occur? What else changed? If the action failed, work out whether the team could not carry out the action or the action itself was based on a mistaken assumption before repeating it.

Carry forward the few lessons that change future decisions. Over time, the review should become easier to prepare because definitions, evidence, and ownership are consistent—not because difficult questions have been removed.

What a better result looks like.

Look for fewer unresolved decisions, fewer conflicting reports, and clearer follow-through alongside the property outcomes. Measure preparation time and action completion if those are real team constraints. Faster reporting is valuable when it releases time for work that improves leasing and the resident experience.
