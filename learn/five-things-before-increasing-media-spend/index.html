Five things to check before increasing media spend.

Before increasing the budget, identify which homes need renters, how many current prospects are likely to lease, and what additional spending could achieve.

By Chris Miller, Founder and CEO, LeaserAbout 6 min read

In a minute.

  • Start with specific available homes and move-in dates.
  • Separate a shortage of demand from a failure to convert it.
  • Judge additional spending against additional outcomes and full costs.
In this article
  1. 1. Identify the homes that need demand.
  2. 2. Account for demand you already have.
  3. 3. Find the point where interest stalls.
  4. 4. Ask what additional leases the money could create.
  5. 5. Compare the extra income with the full extra cost.
  6. Your practical tool

The property isn’t signing enough leases to meet its target. A competitor has increased its advertising. The next recommendation arrives quickly: spend more.

That may be the right decision. But the budget request needs to explain what the next dollar is expected to change. A property can receive plenty of inquiries without attracting renters who want the homes it needs to lease. It can also lose interested renters between a website visit and a completed tour.

Before increasing spend, work through these five checks. They turn a broad request for more leads into a specific decision about availability, conversion, timing, and income.

1. Identify the homes that need demand.

Start with a current list of available homes and their expected ready dates. Separate vacant and ready homes, homes being prepared for new residents, notices to vacate, and homes already matched to signed leases. Keep each unit on the list once. A property-wide occupancy percentage cannot tell you which floor plans need attention next.

For every home without a signed lease, record the expected ready date, asking rent, approved offer, and desired move-in window. Then examine where current advertising sends people. A campaign that mainly attracts one-bedroom interest may be doing little for two-bedrooms that will soon need renters.

The output is a precise need: which homes, how many, and by when. If that need is unclear, the proposed spending increase is still missing its brief.

2. Account for demand you already have.

Review unique, active prospects by stated floor-plan needs, budget, timing, and current stage. Remove duplicate inquiries and closed conversations. Keep signed leases separate from prospects who have not committed, and respect contact preferences.

A long prospect list does not tell you how many people will sign a lease. Some people need a later move-in date; others have already chosen another property. Check which prospects are still looking and who is responsible for following up. A helpful availability update to an interested renter may be more relevant than another broad campaign.

Estimate how many homes would remain unleased if a realistic share of these prospects signs. Base that estimate on similar past inquiries that have had enough time to become leases. Show a range when there are too few examples for a reliable estimate.

3. Find the point where interest stalls.

Follow the journey from inquiry to conversation, booked tour, completed tour, application, signed lease, and move-in. Examine counts as well as percentages. A decline from two leases to one is a different signal from a sustained decline across hundreds of inquiries.

Check whether pricing and availability agree across ads, listings, the website, and leasing conversations. Test the mobile experience and booking path. Review unanswered questions, unavailable tour times, application friction, and reasons people stop responding.

Choose the response that addresses the problem you found. A booking problem needs a booking fix. More traffic may be useful once the path can convert it.

4. Ask what additional leases the money could create.

A channel can receive credit for a lease without being the reason that lease happened. Google’s Conversion Lift documentation describes a controlled comparison between exposed and unexposed groups to estimate additional conversions caused by advertising. [1]

Use a suitable controlled test when scale, eligibility, and measurement allow it. Smaller properties may need a longer observation period or a study across several properties. The comparison must account for renters who encounter advertising for more than one property. A before-and-after improvement alone cannot isolate the advertising effect from seasonality, pricing, or changing availability.

If evidence is limited, say so. Set a maximum test budget, choose the outcome to measure, and agree on a review date. Avoid treating an uncertain attribution report as a spending guarantee.

5. Compare the extra income with the full extra cost.

Use the same evaluation period for the current and proposed plans. Include additional media, execution costs, incentives, and relevant operating costs. Separate genuinely additional leases from move-ins that happen earlier.

When a renter would have leased anyway, the benefit of an earlier move-in is the extra days of rental income, not the entire lease value. Record the assumed alternative move-in date and test what happens if the move-in date improves by fewer days than expected.

Illustrative example · assumptions, not customer results

A budget increase has to earn its place.

Assume a $3,000 campaign causes five households to start paying rent 12 days earlier than they otherwise would. At $2,100 monthly rent and an illustrative 30-day month, that is 60 additional days of rental income across the five homes at $70 per day: $4,200 gross rental income. After media, $1,200 remains before other incremental costs.

If the same five households move in only two days earlier, the modeled income is $700 and the result after media is negative $2,300. These are assumed outcomes, not measured customer results. The calculation shows how strongly the decision depends on timing.

The budget decision starts with the leasing gap the money is meant to close.

CheckWrite down

ConversionThe specific stage losing momentum and the evidence behind that diagnosis.
Additional impactThe expected additional leases or days of rental income, including the range of possible outcomes.
EconomicsExtra income less all extra costs, over one common period.
DecisionIncrease, redirect, or hold; amount; owner; review date; stop condition.

Increase when available homes need more demand and the expected income justifies the added cost. Redirect when demand is aimed at the wrong homes. Hold the increase while fixing an identified bottleneck or collecting the missing evidence.

What a better result looks like.

Track move-ins and rent start dates for the homes that needed attention, along with vacant days and acquisition costs. Give each group of inquiries enough time to become leases before judging its cost per lease. Compare actual outcomes with the assumption that justified the decision. Keep a decision log so the next budget discussion starts with what the team learned.
