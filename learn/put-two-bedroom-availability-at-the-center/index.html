Put two-bedroom availability at the center of the plan.

A practical plan for connecting the homes that need attention with relevant demand, an accurate digital experience, and timely leasing follow-through.

By Chris Miller, Founder and CEO, LeaserAbout 6 min read

In a minute.

  • Start with the homes that need renters and the dates they will be ready.
  • Match messages to the home and the renter’s stated needs.
  • Follow the result through completed tours, signed leases, move-ins, and rent start dates.
In this article

The property is generating interest, but the available two-bedrooms are not leasing. The advertising is broad, the website emphasizes the community, and the leasing report groups every floor plan together.

The next step is to build the plan around the specific homes that need renters. That means more than placing “two-bedroom” in an ad. The message, available dates, tour experience, follow-up, and measurement need to describe the same homes.

This playbook focuses on two-bedroom availability. The same approach works for other unit types: start with confirmed availability and what prospects say they need.

1. Define the availability the plan must cover.

Create a unit-level list with expected ready dates, current status, asking rent, approved offers, and signed-lease commitments. Include upcoming notices, but distinguish a confirmed departure from an unresolved renewal. Confirm uncertain dates with operations.

List the homes that will be available on each date and do not yet have signed leases. A unit with a signed future lease is different from an uncommitted home, but it may still create a period without rent before the start date. Keep both facts visible.

Choose a working window that fits the leasing cycle. Review the list often enough to remove leased homes and update delayed turns. A plan built on last week’s inventory can send useful demand to the wrong place.

2. Establish what the active pipeline can cover.

Group active prospects by the floor plan, price range, and move-in date they have requested, along with their stage in the leasing process. Exclude closed conversations and duplicates. Keep signed leases out of the unsigned pipeline estimate so the same household is not counted twice.

Do not infer a housing need from someone’s demographic profile. Ask what they are looking for and offer relevant options consistently. Federal fair housing protections apply to rental housing; outreach and advertising must also be reviewed against applicable local requirements and platform policies. [1]

Estimate a range of likely move-ins from the remaining prospects using comparable past outcomes. The estimate should show how many homes may still need renters. Avoid presenting a handful of prospects as a certain number of future leases.

Illustrative example · assumptions, not customer results

Fourteen openings can represent several different jobs.

Illustrative scenario: 14 distinct two-bedroom homes are available or expected within 45 days. Four have signed leases aligned to that window, leaving ten uncommitted. Five other active prospects have expressed matching needs and dates; they are not the households behind the four signed leases.

If an assumed 40% of those five prospects move in within the 45-day period, the planning estimate is two more move-ins and eight homes still needing renters. That assumption is a scenario, not a benchmark. If none converts, ten remain; if all five convert, five remain.

The team now has separate jobs: keep the four scheduled move-ins on track, help the five active prospects move forward, and attract suitable interest in the homes that may remain unleased.

3. Make the digital experience answer the real questions.

Use accurate floor plans, current pricing and mandatory cost disclosures, relevant photos, and credible availability dates. Help the visitor understand the layout, differences between available homes, and how to arrange the next step.

Send unit-specific advertising to a relevant destination. If an ad promotes an available two-bedroom, the visitor should not have to search through unrelated units to find it. Check the experience on a phone and verify that a booking or inquiry reaches the right team.

Keep the offer consistent between the advertisement, landing page, listing, and leasing conversation. If details change, update every place a prospect may see them. An attractive message cannot repair contradictory information.

4. Coordinate spend with the leasing team’s capacity.

Examine whether approved spend is attracting demand for homes that need little immediate help. A limited reallocation may be more appropriate than a larger total budget, but only if it does not create an avoidable gap elsewhere.

Confirm that the team can respond, offer useful tour times, and show a ready home or an accurate alternative. Give each unresolved prospect conversation a clear next action. A renter waiting for a price clarification should receive that answer, not another generic reminder.

Set the scope before launch: homes covered, approved spend, current offer, owner, review date, and conditions for pausing. Use authorized data and respect contact permissions when reconnecting with past prospects.

Put it to work

A shared plan for the homes that need renters.

Create one brief that marketing, leasing, and operations can all use.

Planning fieldWhat to include
Homes and datesUnit list; ready dates; signed leases; homes still needing renters.
Relevant demandProspects’ requested homes and dates; stage in the leasing process; duplicate check.
Message and destinationSpecific home benefits, verified price and terms, relevant landing page.
Leasing readinessAvailable tour times, person responsible for follow-up, unanswered questions, and progress preparing homes.
Approved actionSpend allocation, creative or experience changes, outreach scope, approver.
ReviewCompleted tours, applications, signed leases, rent start dates, costs, and homes still needing renters.

Use the same dated brief in the weekly review. Update the inventory first, then adjust the campaign and follow-up plan.

5. Track results for the homes that needed renters.

Monitor inquiries and completed tours for the relevant floor plan, but follow through to signed leases and move-ins. Track when rent begins and what it costs to secure the result. Report cancellations and delays rather than letting them disappear into a top-line lease count.

A stronger result might be fewer unleased homes, earlier rent start dates, or lower acquisition costs for the same outcome. More clicks or a larger lead list cannot establish that by themselves. Check the effect on other homes too: shifting spend can help two-bedrooms while weakening another unit type.

Let the available homes set the brief. Keep the whole property in the decision.

What a better result looks like.

The team can trace a line from a specific availability problem to the message, operational response, and eventual outcome. Ownership gets a clearer explanation of which homes were helped, what was spent, and which assumptions held. The same brief gives the next decision a useful starting point.
