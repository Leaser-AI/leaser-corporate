The warning signs that show up before occupancy drops.

Map upcoming vacancies, move-out dates, and expected move-ins so the team can act while there is still time to change the outcome.

By Chris Miller, Founder and CEO, LeaserAbout 6 min read

In a minute.

  • Today’s occupancy does not show how many homes may be vacant next month.
  • Keep scheduled move-ins with signed leases separate from prospects who have not committed.
  • Give each issue a specific action, owner, and deadline.
In this article
  1. Keep the definitions separate.
  2. Watch departures before they reach the occupancy report.
  3. Check whether expected move-ins can keep pace with departures.
  4. Watch the gap between a departure and rent starting again.
  5. Your practical tool

The occupancy number is still healthy. At the same time, more residents are giving notice, some homes will be ready later than expected, and prospects are taking longer to sign leases.

Those facts can coexist because they describe different moments. Today’s occupied homes tell you where the property stands. Scheduled move-outs and move-ins show where it may be heading.

A useful early-warning view connects those moments. It does not need to predict every renter’s decision. It needs to show which homes may sit vacant, when that could happen, and what the team can do about it.

Keep the definitions separate.

Calculate physical occupancy as occupied homes divided by the total homes included in the report. State that total and explain any changes to it. Keep occupancy, signed future leases, and rent collections as separate measures. A home can be leased for a future date while remaining physically vacant today.

The Census Bureau distinguishes occupied housing from vacant units that are already rented but awaiting occupancy. [1] Your operational report also needs that distinction, even though its property-level calculation is not the same as the Census survey’s rental vacancy rate.

Build the forward view from unit and household records. Transfers within a property change which home is available but do not automatically increase the property’s total number of occupied homes. Across a portfolio, distinguish new demand from a resident moving between your own buildings.

Watch departures before they reach the occupancy report.

Review confirmed notices, unresolved renewals, and known lease expirations by date. An expiring lease is not automatically a move-out; a renewal conversation is not automatically a renewal. Show which renewals remain undecided and assign someone to follow up.

Compare the expected departure pattern with the same property’s recent experience and relevant seasonality. A concentration in one floor plan or week may matter more than a small change in the property-wide rate.

Use resident feedback to identify addressable service issues, without assuming that every complaint predicts departure. The purpose is to respond appropriately and understand which residents may leave, while recognizing that incomplete information cannot establish their intentions.

Check whether expected move-ins can keep pace with departures.

Separate booked tours, completed tours, applications, signed leases, and scheduled move-ins. Use the renter’s intended move-in date alongside the unit’s expected readiness. Interest for two months from now cannot automatically cover next week’s opening.

Track cancellations, repeated reschedules, stalled applications, and the age of unresolved conversations. These are prompts to investigate. They do not all mean demand is falling, and they do not all call for more media.

When estimating how many prospects will sign, use a range based on similar past inquiries that have had enough time to become leases. Show scheduled move-ins with signed leases separately so leadership can see how much of the plan depends on new commitments.

Watch the gap between a departure and rent starting again.

Record expected move-out, completion of apartment preparation, move-in under a signed lease, and the date rent begins for each affected home. Track changes to those dates and their reasons. A construction delay, an unfilled tour slot, and an incentive period create different operating questions.

Check marketing accuracy as dates move. If an unavailable home remains promoted as ready now, the apparent demand problem may begin with an incorrect promise. If a ready home is missing from the website, the property may be suppressing its own visibility.

Prioritize the homes with the most potential lost rental income and problems the team still has time to address. A large dashboard with no one responsible for resolving the problems will not change the timing.

Illustrative example · assumptions, not customer results

A healthy occupancy snapshot can hide a near-term gap.

Illustrative scenario: a property has 300 total units and 285 occupied homes today, or 95% physical occupancy. Over the next 30 days, 24 households are scheduled to leave and 15 new households have signed leases and are scheduled to move in during that period. Assume no transfers, unit-count changes, or timing overlaps.

If those move-outs and move-ins occur as scheduled, the property ends the period with 276 occupied homes: 285 − 24 + 15. That is 92%. If three scheduled move-ins are canceled or delayed beyond the period, the downside case is 273 occupied homes, or 91%, before any additional move-ins from prospects who have not yet signed.

These scenarios show what could happen under the stated assumptions. Returning to 285 occupied homes requires nine additional move-ins in the first scenario or 12 if three scheduled move-ins fall through. The team can now investigate which dates, prospects, and available units can realistically close the gap.

Put it to work

A checklist for upcoming vacancies.

Use the same reporting end date across all data each week and keep the underlying dates available.

SignalQuestion and next action
Confirmed departuresWhich homes and dates are changing? Verify the notice and renewal status.
Scheduled move-insWill the home be ready when the renter plans to move in? Resolve anything that could delay the move.
Prospects without signed leasesWhich active prospects fit the dates? Assign the next useful action.
Delays in the leasing processWhere are tours or applications stalling? Investigate the reason.
Delays preparing homesWhich ready dates have moved? Agree a recovery plan with operations.
Rental income at riskHow much rental income could be lost, and what extra costs could arise? Show assumptions separately.

For each exception, record the home or group of prospects, the owner, the action deadline, and what would trigger escalation. Show both the result if signed leases proceed as scheduled and a scenario with cancellations or delays.

What a better result looks like.

Assess whether the team resolved risks earlier, reduced avoidable vacant days, kept scheduled move-ins on track, and avoided unnecessary emergency spending. Track how far ahead a material issue was identified and whether the assigned action changed it.

Reconcile the forecast with actual outcomes. If the same assumption repeatedly misses, change the assumption. Better forecasting earns trust through visible corrections, not through an ever more precise-looking percentage.
