When the market changes, what should change first?

Respond to a changing market in the right order: check what changed, identify the homes affected, choose an appropriate response, and learn from the result.

By Chris Miller, Founder and CEO, LeaserAbout 5 min read

In a minute.

  • Check whether a market change affects comparable homes and real renter choices.
  • Locate the problem in your own availability and prospect journey.
  • Set the scope, cost, owner, and review conditions before acting.
In this article
  1. Verify the change before reacting to it.
  2. Locate the pressure inside the asset.

A competitor introduces a new offer. Search activity softens. The property signs fewer leases than expected that week. The pressure to change something is immediate, but those signals may describe very different problems.

The right first move depends on what changed, which homes need renters, and where the property is losing momentum. A broad market headline is context. It is not yet an instruction to cut rent or buy more traffic.

Use the sequence below to make a response that is timely, proportionate, and clear enough to evaluate after the pressure has passed.

Verify the change before reacting to it.

Confirm the source, date, scope, and terms of the information. For a competing offer, check the available unit types, lease length, required move-in dates, mandatory fees, and the actual dollar value. Do not compare a limited studio promotion with every two-bedroom at your property.

For your own reports, check tracking, lead definitions, reporting cutoffs, and whether recent inquiries have had enough time to become leases. A sudden fall in inquiries may be a broken form or an import failure. A recent conversion rate may still be incomplete.

Use external market data as context alongside a current comparable set and firsthand prospect feedback. A regional average may cover a different product mix and reporting period from the homes you need to lease.

Locate the pressure inside the asset.

Ask which homes need renters and when those residents need to move in. Check how many active prospects want those homes and move-in dates. Then identify the stage that is changing: visibility, relevant inquiries, tour bookings, attendance, applications, signed leases, or move-ins.

Different patterns suggest different questions. Fewer relevant inquiries may justify a demand response. Stable inquiry volume with fewer completed tours calls for a closer look at access, information, reminders, and competing options. Strong lease signing with delayed move-ins points toward timing and readiness.

Do not infer the cause from the pattern alone. Check the explanation against conversations, operational records, and the actual customer experience. The explanation should be specific enough to tell the team what to change.

Choose a response that fits the problem.

Start with accuracy and access where they are the constraint: current availability, clear pricing, functional booking, and prompt answers. If the issue is a mismatch between demand and inventory, consider changing the message or approved allocation.

When the economics and competitive evidence support a change in rent or concessions, evaluate it directly. Acting in a sensible order does not mean postponing a necessary pricing decision. It means making that decision with the alternatives and full costs visible.

Document dependencies. Marketing cannot promise an earlier move-in without operations confirming readiness. Leasing cannot explain an offer consistently if the approved terms have not reached every prospect touchpoint.

Illustrative example · assumptions, not customer results

One weaker metric does not identify the right remedy.

Illustrative scenario: comparable weekly periods show unique inquiries falling from 40 to 30. Booked tours remain at 12, but completed tours fall from ten to six. Meanwhile, a competitor advertises a new offer for studios; the property’s immediate need is to lease two-bedrooms.

The team checks the attendance gap and finds repeated rescheduling linked to limited available tour slots. That gives the team a reason to add suitable tour appointments before assuming the competitor’s studio offer requires a property-wide concession.

The proposed move is to adjust tour access and provide clear availability information to interested prospects. The team monitors completed tours and later applications, signed leases, and move-ins. The example does not establish that tour access explains every lost renter or that the response will succeed.

Put it to work

An ordered market-response plan.

Give every proposed change a reason and a review condition.

OrderDecision to make
1 / VerifyIs the signal real, current, and relevant to comparable homes?
2 / LocateWhich homes, move-in dates, or steps in the leasing process are affected? What evidence points to the cause?
3 / CompareWhat can accuracy, access, demand, or pricing changes realistically accomplish?
4 / AuthorizeWhat action, budget, terms, and dependencies are approved—and by whom?
5 / ObserveWhich early signals and final outcomes will show whether the response helped?
6 / ReassessContinue, expand, revise, or stop based on the evidence and homes that still need renters.

Decision record: Signal / affected homes / diagnosis / alternatives / chosen action / maximum cost / owner / review date / reversal condition.

Match the review window to the outcome.

A broken link can be checked immediately. A new message can generate early engagement signals within a shorter period than it takes to observe signed leases or rent start dates. Set separate dates for execution checks and outcome checks.

For the illustrative tour-access response, a two-week operating review may reveal whether attendance is recovering. It is an initial review, not a universal test length or proof of a leasing effect. Continue following those prospects for the length of time it normally takes an inquiry to become a lease.

Record other changes during the period. If price, media, availability, and staffing all move together, the result cannot cleanly identify which action helped. Where practical, use a suitable comparison and limit the initial budget and scope until the evidence improves.

A measured response can move quickly. It still needs a reason.

What a better result looks like.

The homes that need renters attract suitable interest, the relevant conversion stage improves, and the property retains more income after the cost of the response. Report what improved, what did not, and how certain the explanation is.

A good decision may also be to hold the plan when the signal proves irrelevant. Record that judgment and its review condition. Resisting an unnecessary concession or budget increase is valuable when the original leasing outcome remains achievable—and the evidence supports it.
