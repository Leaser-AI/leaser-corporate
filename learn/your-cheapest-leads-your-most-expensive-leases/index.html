Your cheapest leads could be your most expensive leases.

A lower cost per inquiry can hide a higher cost per lease. Track the same groups of prospects through to signed leases, using consistent costs and reporting periods.

By Chris Miller, Founder and CEO, LeaserAbout 6 min read

In a minute.

  • Compare groups of inquiries that have had the same amount of time to become leases.
  • Include the costs required to produce and handle the outcome.
  • Separate attributed leases from evidence of additional leases.
In this article
  1. Start by making the comparison fair.
  2. Follow each group of prospects through to move-in.
  3. Count the costs that support the result.
  4. Credit is not the same as contribution.
  5. Your practical tool

One campaign delivers leads for $50. Another delivers them for $100. The first appears twice as efficient—until the leasing team follows those inquiries to a signed lease.

Cost per lead tells you what it cost to generate an inquiry. It does not tell you whether the person is a new prospect, wants an available home, is ready to move, or is likely to sign a lease.

A useful comparison follows the full chain. That can change which channel deserves investment, which needs a better experience, and which apparent saving is creating more work without improving the result.

Start by making the comparison fair.

Choose a consistent lead definition and remove duplicates within and across sources. Keep a record of multiple touchpoints rather than assigning a whole new prospect to each one. Decide how attribution will work before comparing channels.

Compare groups of inquiries received during similar periods, and allow each group the same amount of time to become leases. Counting this week’s leads against this week’s leases mixes different groups. Google notes that conversion delays can make recent acquisition costs look worse before later outcomes are recorded. [1]

Compare results from the same property, with similar available homes and consistent offers where possible. When those conditions differ, describe the difference. A channel supplying studios during a lease-up is not automatically comparable to one supplying two-bedrooms at a stabilized asset.

Follow each group of prospects through to move-in.

Track unique inquiries through meaningful conversations, completed tours, applications, signed leases, and actual move-ins. Choose one primary outcome for the comparison and show the later stages alongside it. A signed lease shows a commitment. The move-in date and the date rent begins show when that commitment becomes occupancy and income.

Record why people leave the process. Price, timing, unit fit, unavailable tours, and an unfinished application suggest different responses. A weak conversion rate can reflect the channel, the offer, the available inventory, or what happens after the inquiry.

Use counts with every percentage. Four outcomes are useful operating evidence, but they are a small sample for predicting the next hundred. Treat a dramatic rate difference cautiously until the comparison has enough time and volume.

Count the costs that support the result.

Include media, listing charges, agency or management fees, relevant creative and software costs, and a consistent allocation of incremental handling work. Separate committed costs from costs that will actually change if the budget changes.

The table’s allocated costs help compare the resources used. They do not mean every allocated dollar can be saved by switching a campaign off. A contracted platform fee or salaried team may remain. State the avoidable cost when proposing a change.

Track incentives and vacancy separately in the economic view rather than quietly including them in one channel’s acquisition number and excluding them from another’s. Otherwise, the apparent winner may just have a different method of counting costs.

Credit is not the same as contribution.

Even a correctly calculated cost per attributed lease does not establish what the channel caused. Some renters encounter several channels before applying; others would have leased without the final paid interaction.

Controlled studies compare outcomes for people exposed to an ad with outcomes for a comparable group that was not exposed, helping estimate the difference the advertising makes. [2] Where those studies are not feasible, be explicit about the limitations of observed attribution. Do not sum overlapping channel credits and present the total as unique property leases.

A channel that looks weak in last-touch reporting may influence an earlier choice. A channel that appears efficient may mostly capture demand already on its way. Both possibilities deserve investigation before a large allocation change.

Buy the outcome you need. Measure the work required to get it.

Change the plan in a way you can learn from.

Investigate the reason for the gap before making a sweeping cut. If one source sends relevant prospects but the landing page is confusing, improve the experience. If another repeatedly sends demand that cannot match available homes, change the targeting approach or reduce the allocation within approved rules.

Make a change with a defined scope and budget and define the review period around the actual leasing cycle. Watch property-wide results as well as channel results so a cleaner attribution report does not conceal lost demand elsewhere.

What a better result looks like.

The property produces the needed leases and move-ins that begin generating rent with less avoidable acquisition cost, or secures more useful outcomes from the same cost. The team can explain the tradeoff with consistent numbers. A higher cost per lead can be acceptable when the complete economics improve.
